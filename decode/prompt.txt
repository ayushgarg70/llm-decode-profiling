The lighthouse at the end of the harbour wall was built in the eighteen-sixties, when the town still lived from fishing and the small coastal steamers that carried timber, salt and letters between the villages along the bay. Its keeper in those years kept a careful logbook, and many of its pages survive. He wrote down the weather at dawn and at dusk, the boats that came in and went out, the price of lamp oil, and every so often a short note about something that had surprised him: a whale close to the rocks, a winter so cold that the inner harbour froze, a ship that arrived three days late with a broken mast and a crew that had not slept.

Reading the logbook today, what stands out is not any single dramatic event but the steady rhythm of the work. The lamp had to be lit at the same minute every evening. The lens had to be cleaned every morning, because a thin film of salt and soot could dim the beam enough that a captain far out at sea might mistake it for a different light. The clockwork that turned the lens had to be wound every few hours through the night. None of these tasks was difficult on its own, but each one had to be done on time, every time, for years, and a single missed step could matter more than a hundred done well.

Later keepers added their own habits. One of them measured the height of the tide against marks he had painted on the harbour wall, and his tables were good enough that the harbour master used them for decades. Another kept a list of the birds that struck the lantern glass during storms, which turned out to be one of the longest continuous records of migration in the region. When the light was finally automated, the last keeper wrote a single line on the final page: the lamp will go on turning without us, and the sea will not notice the difference.

The building still stands. Visitors climb the narrow spiral stairs, look out over the water, and read the old entries in a glass case at the bottom of the tower.
